## 3. METHODS SECTION REWRITE

### Complete Replacement Text (500 Words)

---

**METHODS**

**Study Design and Data Sources**

We conducted a cross-sectional ecological analysis linking census tract-level health outcome estimates with food access indicators across the contiguous United States. Health outcome data were obtained from the CDC PLACES dataset (2023 release), which provides model-based small-area estimates for 29 health measures derived from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System using multilevel regression with poststratification (Zhang et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2017). We focused on five chronic conditions with established food environment associations: obesity (BMI >=30), type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, hypertension, and physical inactivity (Caspi et al., 2012).

Food access data were obtained from the USDA Food Access Research Atlas (FARA, 2019 release), which identifies Low-Income Low-Access (LILA) tracts using poverty rate thresholds and distance to supermarkets (Rhone et al., 2019). We utilized the standard LILA definition (>1 mile urban / >10 miles rural) as our primary classification, with alternative thresholds examined in sensitivity analyses.

**Sample and Exclusions**

We matched 72,531 FARA census tracts to PLACES data using 11-digit GEOIDs, achieving 94% linkage (n=68,170). We applied the following exclusion criteria a priori: (1) tracts with >20% group quarters population to avoid institutional populations (prisons, military, dormitories) that confound community-level interpretation (n=278 excluded); (2) tracts with extreme outliers (>5 SD) on any health outcome suggesting data quality concerns (n=58 excluded); (3) Washington DC, given its unique urban structure as a non-state jurisdiction, was retained in primary analyses but examined separately in sensitivity analyses. Final analytic sample: 67,834 census tracts representing approximately 210 million residents.

**Outcome Measures**

We constructed a composite health burden index by calculating the mean z-score across five health outcomes (standardized to the national mean), yielding a single continuous measure where positive values indicate higher-than-average chronic disease burden. Internal consistency was adequate (Cronbach's alpha = 0.87).

**Statistical Analysis**

We estimated expected health burden conditional on food access characteristics using ordinary least squares regression with state fixed effects to account for unobserved policy and environmental differences:

Burden_i = beta_0 + beta_1*LILA_i + beta_2*LowIncome_i + beta_3*Rural_i + beta_4*NoVehicle_i + State_FE + epsilon_i

Resilience scores were calculated as inverted standardized residuals from this model, where positive values indicate better-than-predicted health outcomes. Tracts exceeding the 90th percentile of the national resilience distribution were classified as "high resilience."

**Sensitivity Analyses**

We assessed robustness through: (1) alternative LILA definitions (0.5+10 mile, 1+20 mile, vehicle-adjusted); (2) quantile regression at the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles; (3) bootstrap standard errors (1,000 replications); (4) analysis excluding DC; (5) stratification by urban/rural status and census region. Spatial autocorrelation was assessed using Moran's I calculated from queen contiguity weights.

**Post-Hoc Power Analysis**

With n=67,834 tracts, our analysis had >99% power to detect standardized effect sizes of d=0.02 at alpha=0.05, well below the observed LILA coefficient (d=0.31). However, we acknowledge that statistical power does not address threats to internal validity inherent in ecological designs (Oakes, 2004).

**Software**

Analyses were conducted using Python 3.13 (pandas, scipy, statsmodels) with geographic processing in Go 1.21. Reproducible code is available at [repository URL].

## 4. THE DC PROBLEM: Resolution Strategy

### Option A: Exclude DC Entirely (RECOMMENDED)

**Justification Paragraph for Methods:**

> We excluded Washington DC from primary analyses due to its unique jurisdictional status as a city-state with demographic, economic, and health care delivery characteristics that are non-comparable to state-based census tracts. DC's 205 census tracts (of which 23 are LILA) serve a population of 712,000 within 68 square miles, yielding population densities exceeding 10,000/square mile in most tracts. The absence of suburban census tracts that exist within every U.S. state creates selection effects that bias state-level comparisons. Additionally, DC's Medicaid expansion, unique federal employee population, and concentrated healthcare infrastructure represent confounders not generalizable to the 50 states. Sensitivity analyses including DC did not substantively alter conclusions (see Supplementary Table S3).

### Option B: Include with Massive Caveats

**Results Paragraph if DC Retained:**

> Among state-level comparisons, Washington DC emerged as an extreme outlier, ranking last in mean LILA tract resilience (mean = -0.60, SD = 1.12) despite having the highest per-capita healthcare expenditure of any U.S. jurisdiction. This finding requires cautious interpretation: DC is the only jurisdiction without suburban or rural census tracts, its 712,000 population is served by a disproportionate concentration of tertiary care centers creating potential spatial spillover effects, and its unique governance structure limits comparability to state-administered Medicaid programs. We present DC separately in Table 4 and exclude it from rankings to avoid misleading policy conclusions. The DC paradox—high resources, poor outcomes—warrants dedicated investigation beyond this study's scope.

### Recommended Approach

**Primary analysis:** Exclude DC
**Supplementary analysis:** Full results including DC in Appendix
**Discussion:** Note DC as illustration of ecological fallacy risk

## 7. REVISED ABSTRACT (Copy-Paste Ready)

---

**Background:** The relationship between geographic food access and chronic disease exhibits substantial heterogeneity, yet research predominantly focuses on vulnerability rather than positive deviance. We examined whether residual-based methods could systematically identify census tracts with better-than-expected health outcomes despite food insecurity classification.

**Methods:** We conducted a cross-sectional ecological analysis linking CDC PLACES 2023 tract-level health estimates (obesity, diabetes, coronary heart disease, hypertension, physical inactivity) with USDA Food Access Research Atlas 2019 Low-Income Low-Access (LILA) classifications for 67,834 census tracts across 50 U.S. states. Using ordinary least squares regression with state fixed effects, we modeled expected health burden and calculated standardized residuals to identify positive outliers. Sensitivity analyses examined alternative LILA thresholds and excluded special populations.

**Results:** Among 8,734 LILA tracts meeting inclusion criteria, 1,059 (12.1%) demonstrated resilience scores exceeding the national 90th percentile, with health outcomes 0.6 to 4.7 standard deviations below model predictions. Geographic clustering emerged in the Southeast and upper Midwest. Classification stability across LILA definitions reached 78% for high-resilience tracts. However, racial disparities persisted: resilient LILA tracts were disproportionately white (69% vs. 29% in low-resilience LILA tracts), and majority-minority LILA tracts showed mean resilience 0.4 SD below majority-white counterparts (p<0.001).

**Conclusions:** Residual-based positive deviance methods can identify communities exceeding health predictions within food-insecure areas, generating hypotheses for qualitative investigation. However, cross-sectional ecological data cannot establish protective mechanisms, and the observed racial patterning suggests structural factors beyond food access shape these outcomes. Mixed-methods research with community engagement is needed before policy implications can be drawn.
