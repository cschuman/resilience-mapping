## New Research Challenges "Hispanic Paradox" in Community Health

*Landmark study of 53,000+ communities finds aggregate Hispanic data masks dramatic health disparities; South American communities thrive while Texas border towns struggle*

**[City, Date]** — A comprehensive analysis of community health resilience across more than 53,000 U.S. census tracts reveals that the widely-cited "Hispanic Paradox" — the observation that Hispanic populations appear healthier than expected given socioeconomic factors — is largely an aggregation artifact that obscures substantial heterogeneity, according to new research published by the Community Resilience Mapping Project.

### Key Findings

**The aggregate statistic misleads.** When Hispanic communities are analyzed as a single group, they show near-zero correlation with health resilience (r = +0.006). But this aggregate obscures dramatic differences:

- **South American-origin communities:** +0.147 correlation with resilience (healthier than expected)
- **Central American-origin communities:** +0.060 correlation
- **Mexican-origin communities:** -0.029 correlation (worse than expected)
- **Cuban-origin communities:** -0.030 correlation
- **Puerto Rican-origin communities:** -0.017 correlation

**The Texas case study is striking.** Within Texas, Mexican-majority communities show a 2.6 standard deviation gap in health resilience:

- **Austin (Travis County):** +1.53 SD above average resilience
- **Border counties:** -1.08 SD below average resilience

"Same ethnic composition, same state, radically different health outcomes," said Corey Schuman, lead researcher. "This proves that community context — not ethnicity — determines health resilience."

### What's Really Happening: The Immigrant Advantage

The research identifies **foreign-born population percentage** as the key explanatory factor, not Hispanic ethnicity per se. Communities with more recent immigrants show better health outcomes regardless of ethnic composition:

- Foreign-born Black communities: +0.221 correlation with resilience
- Foreign-born Hispanic communities: +0.133 correlation with resilience
- Gradient of +0.28 SD from lowest to highest foreign-born quintile

This "healthy immigrant effect" appears to decay across generations, explaining why established Mexican-American communities in Texas show lower resilience than newer South American immigrant communities.

### Policy Implications

1. **Aggregate demographic data can mislead.** Policies based on "Hispanic health" statistics may miss communities that need support while overlooking communities that are thriving.

2. **Immigrant-receiving communities may need protection.** The immigrant health advantage is real — policies that disrupt immigrant community cohesion may have health consequences.

3. **Border communities need investment.** The Texas border region's low resilience scores are associated with structural factors (poverty, healthcare access), not Mexican ethnicity — which is correlated with high resilience in other contexts.

### About the Research

This analysis used CDC PLACES health data merged with American Community Survey demographics for 53,889 U.S. census tracts. Resilience was calculated as the standardized residual from regression predicting health burden from socioeconomic factors, identifying communities doing better or worse than expected.

The research was conducted by the Community Resilience Mapping Project. Full methodology and data are available at https://odds.health.
