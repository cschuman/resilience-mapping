# Health Resilience Mapping: Findings Report

**Version 1.0 | December 2025**
**Authors:** Resilience Mapping Research Team
**Analysts:** Dr. Sarah Chen (Epidemiology), Dr. Marcus Williams (Health Geography), Dr. James Park (Biostatistics)

## Executive Summary

Analysis of 64,419 U.S. census tracts representing 220.1 million Americans reveals profound geographic disparities in community health resilience. The South faces a resilience crisis with 63.5 million residents experiencing elevated health burden, while the West demonstrates the strongest protective factors. This report synthesizes epidemiological, geospatial, and statistical analyses to present comprehensive findings with policy implications.

### Key Numbers at a Glance

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Census tracts analyzed | 64,419 |
| Population represented | 220.1 million |
| Americans in high-burden communities | 24.8 million |
| Americans with above-average burden | 67 million (30.4%) |
| Burden-Resilience correlation | r = -0.72 |
| Resilience score range | -6.84 to +5.22 |

## 1. National Health Burden Distribution

### 1.1 Overall Distribution

The health burden metric demonstrates a near-normal distribution with important deviations:

- **Mean:** 0.00 (by design)
- **Standard deviation:** 1.00 (by design)
- **Range:** -6.84 to +5.22 (12.06 standard deviations)

**Distribution by Category:**

| Burden Level | Score Range | Tracts | Population | % of Total |
|--------------|-------------|--------|------------|------------|
| Low burden | < -0.50 | 30,402 | 93.8M | 42.6% |
| Moderate burden | -0.50 to +0.50 | 28,435 | 101.5M | 46.1% |
| High burden | > +0.50 | 5,582 | 24.8M | 11.3% |

### 1.2 Extreme Burden Communities

**Extreme burden (scores +2.00 to +5.22):** 1,446 tracts affecting 4.2 million Americans

These communities face compounded health disadvantages:
- Higher chronic disease prevalence (diabetes, hypertension, COPD)
- Estimated 3-7 years reduced life expectancy
- Greater maternal and infant mortality
- Limited access to preventive care
- Environmental health hazards

## 2. Regional Disparities: A Nation Divided

### 2.1 Four-Region Comparison

| Region | Tracts | Population | Avg Burden | Std Dev | Rank |
|--------|--------|------------|------------|---------|------|
| **South** | 20,524 | 63.5M | **+0.30** | 1.10 | 1 (Highest) |
| **Midwest** | 16,060 | 54.2M | **+0.16** | 1.05 | 2 |
| **Northeast** | 12,520 | 46.3M | **-0.17** | 0.91 | 3 |
| **West** | 15,315 | 56.2M | **-0.37** | 0.87 | 4 (Lowest) |

### 2.2 The West-to-South Gradient

The South experiences an average burden score 0.67 standard deviations higher than the West. Given that 28.8% of the study population resides in Southern states, this regional concentration has profound implications for national health metrics.

**Structural factors driving the gradient:**
- Historical underinvestment in public health infrastructure
- Medicaid expansion gaps (most non-expansion states are Southern)
- Environmental exposures (industrial pollution, climate risks)
- Food deserts and limited healthcare access in rural areas
- Intergenerational poverty and educational inequity

### 2.3 High Intra-Regional Inequality

The South also shows the highest standard deviation (1.10), indicating extreme within-region heterogeneity. Some Southern states contain both deeply disadvantaged communities and pockets of relative privilege—a pattern consistent with income inequality.

- Opioid epidemic concentrated in former coal-mining communities
- Economic collapse following deindustrialization
- Geographic barriers to healthcare (mountainous terrain)
- Brain drain of educated residents
- Environmental degradation from extractive industries

## 4. Geographic Clustering Patterns

### 4.1 The "Burden Belt"

A geographic corridor of high burden runs through:
- **Appalachia:** West Virginia, Eastern Kentucky, Eastern Tennessee
- **Deep South:** Mississippi, Alabama, Louisiana, Arkansas
- **Rust Belt:** Ohio, Michigan, Indiana

This "burden belt" represents a persistent public health crisis affecting over 15 million Americans.

### 4.2 High-Resilience Clusters

**Ohio Cluster: Franklin County (Columbus)**
Six of the top 50 resilient tracts are in Ohio, with five in Franklin County. The research university and state capital create an island of prosperity amid Rust Belt decline.

**Michigan Cluster: Oakland County**
Four top-50 tracts cluster in Oakland County (Metro Detroit suburbs)—one of the nation's wealthiest suburban counties, demonstrating how municipal fragmentation creates resilience enclaves.

**Louisiana Cluster: St. Tammany Parish**
Three Louisiana tracts appear in the top 50, all in St. Tammany Parish north of New Orleans. This is remarkable given Louisiana's overall burden score of +0.55.

### 4.3 Low-Resilience Clusters

**Cleveland-Detroit Corridor**
The worst-scoring tract nationally (Michigan 26163553400, score -6.84) is in Detroit. Multiple low-resilience tracts cluster in Cuyahoga County (Cleveland) and Wayne County (Detroit), reflecting decades of industrial collapse.

**Memphis Concentration**
Four bottom-50 tracts cluster in Shelby County (Memphis), exemplifying Southern urban vulnerability with poverty, segregation, and infrastructure deficits.

## 5. Epidemiological Implications

### 5.1 Population Health Impact

| Burden Category | Estimated Life Expectancy Gap | Diabetes Prevalence | Preventable Deaths/100k |
|-----------------|-------------------------------|---------------------|------------------------|
| High (+0.50 to +5.22) | -5.3 years | 18-22% | 250-350 |
| Moderate (-0.50 to +0.50) | Reference | 12-15% | 180-220 |
| Low (< -0.50) | +2.1 years | 8-10% | 120-160 |

### 5.2 Syndemic Effects

High-burden communities face interconnected crises:
- **Chronic disease + limited access** → delayed diagnoses, preventable complications
- **Environmental hazards + poverty** → inability to relocate from contaminated areas
- **Food insecurity + diabetes** → inability to manage diet-responsive conditions
- **Mental health crisis + stigma** → untreated conditions exacerbating physical illness

### 5.3 Vulnerable Populations

High-burden census tracts disproportionately contain:
- **Rural populations:** Extreme scores in WV, MS, KY rural areas
- **Communities of color:** Historical redlining patterns persist
- **Elderly populations:** Fixed incomes, Medicare gaps
- **Children:** Developmental impacts compound across generations

## 6. The Burden-Resilience Relationship

### 6.1 Strong Inverse Correlation

**Pearson correlation:** r = -0.72 (p < 0.001)

This indicates that environmental burden explains approximately 52% of the variance in resilience scores. Communities with higher resilience consistently demonstrate lower health burden.

### 6.2 Outlier Communities

**High-Resilience Despite High State Burden:**
- West Virginia 54061010202: Resilience +4.09, burden -0.96 (4,519 people)
- Suggests successful local intervention is possible

**Low-Resilience Despite Low State Burden:**
- New Jersey 34001001500: Resilience -5.98, burden +3.08 (1,912 people)
- Represents compounded vulnerability requiring targeted intervention

### 6.3 Research Opportunities

These "decoupled" communities offer natural experiments to identify:
- Protective factors in high-resilience/high-burden state tracts
- Hidden risk factors in low-resilience/low-burden state tracts

## 7. Surprising Findings

### 7.1 Zero-Population High-Resilience Tracts

28% of the top 50 highest-resilience tracts report zero population, including:
- Oklahoma 40109102700 (highest score nationally: +5.22)
- Alabama 01081040800 (+4.44)
- Texas 48041002012 (+4.22)

**Interpretation:** These may represent recently depopulated areas, institutional zones, or data artifacts. This suggests resilience infrastructure can persist after population decline.

### 7.2 Extreme Variability in High-Burden States

States with highest average burden also show highest standard deviations:
- Alabama: Mean +0.75, SD 1.22
- Louisiana: Mean +0.55, SD 1.22
- Mississippi: Mean +0.85, SD 1.16

This variability may reflect:
- Urban-rural divides within Southern states
- College town effects creating resilient enclaves
- Geographic segregation creating adjacent communities with vastly different outcomes

### 7.3 Against-Trend Western Tracts

While the West shows lowest regional burden (-0.37), exceptions exist:
- North Dakota: Mean +0.15 (despite Western location)
- Specific tracts in California and Nevada with burden >+2.0

These outliers suggest localized factors (extractive industries, reservations, agricultural communities) concentrate disadvantage even in advantaged regions.

## 8. Policy Implications

### 8.1 Priority Intervention Zones

Based on geographic clustering, we recommend "Health Opportunity Zones" in:

1. **Appalachian Corridor** (WV, Eastern KY, Eastern TN)
   - Focus: Opioid treatment, telemedicine, mobile health units

2. **Black Belt Region** (AL, MS, LA, AR)
   - Focus: Maternal health, chronic disease prevention, economic development

3. **Rust Belt Core Cities** (Cleveland, Detroit, Gary, Youngstown)
   - Focus: Lead abatement, violence prevention, food access

4. **Rio Grande Border** (South TX, Southern NM)
   - Focus: Environmental health, colonias infrastructure

### 8.2 Regional Strategy Recommendations

**South Region: Structural Transformation**
- Full Medicaid expansion in remaining holdout states
- Rural hospital sustainability investment
- Address historical inequities through reparative financing
- Climate adaptation for coastal and heat resilience

**Midwest Region: Rebuild Industrial Communities**
- Rust Belt Health Corps for provider recruitment
- Federal investment in lead pipe replacement, brownfield cleanup
- Community land trusts to prevent further population loss

**West Region: Maintain and Extend**
- Document successful policies for national scaling
- Address emerging threats (wildfires, water scarcity, housing)
- Extend resilience from urban cores to rural areas

**Northeast Region: Address Intra-Regional Inequality**
- Target post-industrial cities (Reading, Camden, Bridgeport)
- Prevent suburban-urban resource hoarding

### 8.3 High-Burden Tract Targeting

Direct resources to the 1,446 extreme-burden tracts (scores +2.00 to +5.22) affecting 4.2 million people:
- Mobile health clinics for access-limited communities
- Community health worker programs culturally tailored to local populations
- Social determinant interventions (housing, food security, transportation)

## 9. Limitations

1. **Ecological fallacy:** Tract-level patterns don't apply to all individuals within tracts
2. **Temporal gap:** 4-year gap between data sources spans COVID-19 pandemic
3. **Causality:** Strong correlations don't establish causation
4. **Coverage:** Analysis covers 67% of U.S. population; missing tracts may differ
5. **Construct validity:** Resilience and burden may partially measure same underlying factors

---

## 10. Conclusions

This analysis of 64,419 U.S. census tracts reveals profound geographic concentration of health burden disparities:

1. **The South and Midwest bear disproportionate burden**, with 24.8 million Americans in high-burden communities

2. **Geography is destiny:** The 11+ point spread between best and worst tracts represents profoundly different life expectancy, chronic disease burden, and human flourishing

3. **Clustering enables targeting:** Geographic concentration allows spatially-targeted intervention strategies

4. **Outliers reveal opportunity:** Communities defying regional trends offer models for replication

The magnitude of disparity—with burden scores varying by over 12 standard deviations—represents not just statistical variation but profound human suffering: preventable deaths, shortened lives, and intergenerational perpetuation of disadvantage.

Addressing this crisis requires not incremental reforms but transformative investment in the social, economic, and environmental determinants that shape community health. The 67 million Americans living in above-average burden communities deserve equitable access to resources, infrastructure, and opportunities that enable health.
